# Write like me

## When to use

Two separate jobs live in this one skill.

**Learn mode.** The person wants you to study how they write and save it, so every future draft sounds like them. Triggered by things like "learn my writing style" or "read my sent emails and learn how I write".

**Write mode.** The person wants something drafted in their voice, using whatever you've already learned. Triggered by "write this like I would", "draft this in my voice", or just by any other skill that needs to produce a draft.

## What you need

- For learn mode: permission to read either 10 to 20 of their recent sent emails, or a handful of examples they paste in directly.
- For write mode: context/voice.md (may still say it hasn't been filled in; that's fine, see below).

## Learn mode: steps

1. **Ask permission explicitly.** Don't read anyone's sent mail without being asked to. Say something like: "To learn your writing style I'd want to look at 10 to 20 of your recent sent emails. Are you happy for me to do that, or would you rather paste in some examples?"
2. **Read what they allow.** Either the sent emails from their connected inbox, or the examples they paste in. Favour emails to a range of people (a client, a supplier, a colleague) over emails all to one person, so you catch how they vary by audience.
3. **Extract patterns, not quotes.** Look for:
   - How they greet people (first name only, "Hi", "Hey", nothing at all)
   - How they sign off, and whether that changes by audience
   - Roughly how long their sentences and paragraphs run
   - How formal they are, and whether that shifts between a client and a supplier
   - Words and phrases they use often
   - Words or phrases they clearly never use
   - How they open an email (straight to the point, a line of small talk, a question)
   - How they close (a concrete next step, nothing at all, a question)
4. **Write it up as a short summary**, not as a copy of their emails. Something like: "You tend to open with the person's first name and no greeting word. Short sentences, often under 12 words. You sign off with 'Cheers' to colleagues and 'Many thanks' to clients. You never use exclamation marks. With clients you're more formal; with your team you're blunter and quicker."
5. **Show them the summary and ask them to correct it.** They know their own voice better than any pattern-matching does. Change anything they flag.
6. **Save the corrected version into context/voice.md.** Keep it as plain, short notes under clear headings (greeting, sign-off, sentence length, formality by audience, words used, words avoided, example lines), not as a long essay.
7. **Never store the original emails themselves**, only the patterns you pulled from them. If they pasted examples in in the chat, don't copy full examples into context/voice.md; summarise what the examples show.

## Write mode: steps

1. Read context/voice.md. If it's filled in, use it directly: match the greeting, sign-off, sentence length and formality it describes.
2. If context/voice.md still says it hasn't been filled in, or doesn't exist, say so plainly and offer to run learn mode. Then draft anyway using the plain, human default from human-email, and flag that it's a best guess, not their actual voice.
3. Check context/people.md for who the email is going to, so formality matches the relationship (a long-standing client gets a different register from a new supplier).
4. Draft the piece.
5. Run human-email's checklist against the draft before showing it. Voice and "sounds human" are two different checks; both have to pass.
6. Show the draft and say plainly that it's a draft to review, not something sent.

## Rules

- Never invent a quote and present it as something the person has actually said or written before. Patterns are fine to infer; fake examples attributed to them are not.
- Only read what the person has given permission for. If they haven't said yes to reading their sent mail, don't go looking.
- Store patterns, never copies of private emails. context/voice.md should never contain someone else's personal information lifted from an email thread.
- If the person's own writing uses something that human-email would normally flag (for instance, they genuinely do use exclamation marks in certain places), their actual habit wins over the general rule for things that are a matter of taste, not for the worst tells (no em dashes, no negative parallelism, no stock vocabulary ever survive, however the examples looked).
- Keep context/voice.md honest about gaps. If 10 emails only showed how they write to clients and never to suppliers, say that, rather than guessing at the missing half.

## If something is missing

If there's no context/voice.md and no permission yet to learn one, write mode still works: use the plain, human default from human-email, tell the person what's missing, and offer to run learn mode properly when they have a few minutes.
