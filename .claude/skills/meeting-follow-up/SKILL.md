# Meeting follow-up

## When to use

After any meeting where the person has notes, a transcript or a recording summary and wants it turned into something useful: a short write-up, a follow-up email, and a note of anything they personally need to do.

## What you need

- The raw material: pasted notes, a transcript, or a summary from a recording tool.
- context/people.md, to know who was in the room and how formal the follow-up email should be.
- context/voice.md and human-email, for the follow-up email itself.
- The meeting's name or subject and the date, for labelling tasks and any saved notes.

## Steps

1. **Read the raw material properly.** Don't skim. Meetings often bury the actual decision halfway through a tangent.
2. **Write a short summary** with three parts: decisions made, actions (each with an owner and a date if one was given), and open questions still unresolved. Keep it plain and short, no headers needed for a short meeting.
3. **Draft the follow-up email** in the person's voice, following write-like-me and human-email. State the decisions and the actions plainly, say who's doing what, and don't recap the whole meeting line by line; the people on the call were there.
4. **Pull out the person's own actions** (not other people's) and propose each as a suggested task in the task list. Mark each one suggested, with addedBy claude, and a source noting it came from this meeting, including the meeting's name and date. Don't add other attendees' actions as the person's tasks.
5. **Ask before saving the notes anywhere.** Offer to save the write-up into files, in the client's folder if it's client work or the project folder otherwise, named with the date and meeting, for example "2026-10-06 Meeting with Hollins Lettings.md". Only save it if they say yes.
6. **Present all three together**: the summary, the draft email, and the list of tasks you're suggesting, so the person can approve or adjust in one go.

## Rules

- Never send the follow-up email. Draft only.
- Never add a suggested task for anyone except the person themselves; other attendees' actions belong in the summary, not the task list.
- Always run human-email's checklist on the draft email before showing it.
- Don't save notes anywhere without an explicit yes. If they say nothing, treat that as no and just show the summary.
- If the meeting covered something sensitive (a difficult client conversation, a staffing issue), say so before drafting anything, and ask if they still want a written follow-up sent.

## Examples

Short internal meeting, no email needed: "From your notes, 2 decisions: you're moving the Hollins Lettings job to next Tuesday, and Sam's taking the invoicing off your plate from next month. One open question: whether the Marlow Dental quote needs revising before Friday. I've added 'Confirm Hollins Lettings job for Tuesday' and 'Hand invoicing over to Sam' to your task list as suggestions. Want a follow-up email as well, or just the task list?"

Client call needing a follow-up: "Here's the summary from your call with Tom at Greenway Café: he's confirmed the Saturday booking for 30 people, and you agreed to send a revised menu by Wednesday. Draft email below, and I've suggested a task 'Send revised menu to Greenway Café' due Wednesday."

## If something is missing

If the notes don't give an owner or date for an action, say so in the summary rather than guessing, and ask the person to confirm before it goes into a task with a due date.
