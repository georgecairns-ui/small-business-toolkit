# Human email

## Why it matters

People can spot a machine-written email now, and when they do, trust drops immediately. It doesn't matter how correct or polite the email is. The moment it reads as generated, the reader assumes nobody actually thought about them, and anything the email is asking for gets harder.

This is the most important skill in this kit. Every other skill that drafts an email (draft-a-reply, polite-chaser, meeting-follow-up, delegate-it) ends with the checklist below. Do not skip it because the draft "looks fine". Machine writing always looks fine. That is the problem.

## Fix structure before words

Swapping out a few words for plainer ones is not enough. The tells that give an email away are mostly structural: the shape of the sentences, what the email does in its first line, how it ends. Fix the shape first, then the vocabulary.

## The main tells, with before and after

**"It's not X, it's Y" contrasts.** This is the single biggest giveaway.
Before: "It's not that the invoice is wrong, it's that it's late."
After: "The invoice is fine. It's just late."

**Lists of three.** Machines default to three items because it feels tidy.
Before: "It's quicker, cheaper and easier to manage."
After: "It's quicker and it costs less."

**Every sentence the same length.** Real writing has short sentences next to long ones.
Before: "I checked the file. I found the issue. I fixed it today. I'll send it over."
After: "Found it. Took a bit of digging, but it's fixed, and I'll send the file over today."

**Opening by restating their email back to them.** Wastes the reader's time; they already know what they wrote.
Before: "Thanks for your email about the delivery date for the Marlow Dental order."
After: "The Marlow Dental order ships Thursday."

**Announcing what you're about to say.** Nobody does this out loud.
Before: "A couple of things worth flagging before you look at the quote."
After: just say the two things.

**Over-hedging.** Softening every sentence until nothing is actually said.
Before: "I think it might possibly be worth considering a slightly later date."
After: "Let's push it back a week."

**Flattery openers.** "Great question", "I hope this finds you well" and similar read as a form letter, not a person.
Before: "Great question, and I hope you're having a good week."
After: skip straight to the answer.

**Summary-recap closers.** Repeating what the email just said.
Before: "So to sum up, we'll send the invoice Friday and follow up next week."
After: stop after the last point. The reader was there for the whole email.

**Bold words and bullets in a normal email.** Fine in a report, wrong in an email under a few hundred words; it reads like a slide, not a message.
Before: "**Key points:** - Price confirmed - Delivery Thursday"
After: "The price is confirmed and it'll arrive Thursday."

**Em dashes.** Never use one, in a draft or in an example. Use a comma, a full stop or a hyphen instead.
Before: "The order's ready [long dash] we just need your sign off." (the long dash is the giveaway)
After: "The order's ready. We just need your sign off."

**Stock vocabulary.** Words like delve, leverage, seamless, robust, crucial, navigate, landscape, tapestry and similar read as generated, whatever the context.
Before: "This should help us navigate the busy season more seamlessly."
After: "This should make the busy season easier."

## What human email does instead

- Gets to the point in the first line. No throat-clearing.
- Varies sentence length on purpose. A short one, then a longer one.
- Includes one concrete detail: a name, a date, an amount, something specific to this exact situation. This is the hardest thing for a machine to fake, so it is the strongest signal of a real person.
- Has a clear ask, with a date attached if there is one. "Can you confirm by Friday" beats "let me know your thoughts".
- Is short. Most business emails are too long, not too short.
- Signs off in a way that matches the person it's from and the person it's going to. Check context/voice.md for how this person actually signs off; don't invent one.

## Length guide by email type

| Type of email | Rough length |
|---|---|
| Reply to a short email (a few lines) | A few lines back, not more |
| Reply to a detailed email | Match each point briefly, no headers needed |
| A chase or reminder | Short. Specifics, then the ask |
| Cold or first-contact email | Under 150 words |
| A note that covers something genuinely complex | As long as it needs to be, but never pad it out |

If in doubt, cut the draft down rather than add to it. Length is one of the biggest tells on its own.

## Using context/voice.md

If context/voice.md exists and has been filled in, it tells you how this specific person writes: their usual greeting, their sign-off, roughly how long their sentences run, words they use and words they'd never use, and how formal they get with different people. Use it. Match it exactly rather than guessing at a generic "professional" tone.

If context/voice.md is missing or still says it hasn't been filled in, say so plainly: "I don't have your voice notes yet, so this is my best guess at a plain, human draft. Running write-like-me's learn mode would make this sound more like you specifically." Draft anyway using the rules above; don't block on it.

## Final checklist

Before showing a draft, check every one of these:

1. Does it answer or say the thing in the first line, with no recap of the other person's message?
2. Is every "it's not X, it's Y" style contrast removed?
3. Is there no list of exactly three items used for effect?
4. Does sentence length vary, not sit at one steady rhythm?
5. Is there at least one concrete, specific detail?
6. Is there a clear ask, with a date if one is needed?
7. Is it as short as it can be for what it needs to do?
8. Is the sign-off one this person would actually use?
9. Zero em dashes, zero bold text or bullets (unless it's a genuine list of 4 or more items or options)?
10. Zero stock words: delve, leverage, seamless, robust, crucial, navigate, landscape, tapestry, or similar?

If any answer is no, fix it before showing the person. The person sends the email themselves; you only ever draft it.

## Before and after example

Before (reads machine-written):
"Hi Priya, I hope this email finds you well. Thanks for reaching out about the catering order for next week. I wanted to let you know that it's not that we can't do Saturday, it's that our usual supplier is fully booked, so we'd need to push to Sunday instead. A few things worth flagging: the price stays the same, the menu stays the same, and delivery stays at 11am. Let me know your thoughts, and thanks again for your patience. Best regards."

After (reads human):
"Hi Priya, Saturday's a problem because our usual supplier is booked solid. Sunday works though, at the same price and still at 11am. Does Sunday suit you? If so I'll lock it in today. Speak soon."

## If something is missing

If you don't know how this person signs off, or how formal they are with this particular reader, check context/people.md for the relationship and context/voice.md for their general style. If neither has an answer, use a plain, warm, moderately formal default and say you've guessed, so the person can correct it.
